\chapter{Results}\label{chapter:results}
  The following chapter deals with evaluating the performance and results of the classification performed on both the \ac{DEAP} dataset and the Headset dataset. For each dataset, the same analysis approach is followed. Firstly, the accuracies of each model are analysed alongside the f1-score for each subject/session with a focus on variance between those values. Secondly, since the hyperparameters are optimized according to each subject/session, the distribution of those parameters\footnote{The hyperparameter space can be found in \autoref{tab:svm_hyperparam_space}} is further investigated to find certain trends within these models. Thirdly, normalised confusion matrices are taken as a measure to get insights of the actual predictions of each model. Apart from a global confusion matrix originating from all confusion matrices combined, the best and worst classifier's matrices are explored as well.
  \section{DEAP}
    % Accuracies
    The mean and balanced accuracies of each subject are illustrated in \autoref{fig:deap_acc}. It is notable that both mean and balanced accuracy are very close to each other with 68.3\% and 68.7\% respectively, concluding that each model makes overall balanced predictions although it is trained on an imbalanced dataset. Additionally, both the mean and balanced accuracy occupy a large range. While the mean accuracy ranges between 58\% and 83\%, the balanced accuracy varies from 59\% to 82\%. This suggests that there are many differences between subjects in \ac{EEG} patterns and quality in data. Nonetheless, each model is able to perform classifications above chance level, suggesting that the model actually learns patterns that are significant for \ac{MGR} in this case.\par

    % f1-scores
    In terms of f1-score, the distribution of results follows a similar pattern like the outcomes of the accuracies, illustrated in \autoref{fig:deap_f1}. With a mean value of 0.662 and values ranging from 0.55 to 0.82, the findings vary across subjects. Nonetheless, since the f1-score expresses the balance between precision and recall, the models have a reasonable well performance with flunctuating scores depending on the subject. Reasons for that could be noise or subject-dependent features. \par

    % Parameter distribution
    The overall hyperparameter distribution is showcased in \autoref{fig:deap_hyperparams_raw}, illustrating the count of each value appearing in an optimal configuration for the classification pipeline. It becomes obvious that the sigmoid function is the optimal kernel for most of the subjects. For C regularization, the majority of models utilize a value of 10.0, indicating the need to fit the training data more strictly for desirable results on the test validation dataset. Regarding the gamma values used, it is noticable that the \textit{auto} option is not in use, suggesting that it is not competitive compared to more robust options like \textit{scale}. The absolute distribution of the feature count, however, does not give away any trend in favor of performance when looking at the overall count. \par
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/chapter06/deap/deap_hyperparams_2x2.pdf}
      \caption{The distribution of optimal hyperparameters across all subjects presented in absolute counts}
      \label{fig:deap_hyperparams_raw}
    \end{figure}
    To further explore the best possible hyperparameters for \ac{MGR} in the \ac{DEAP} dataset, \autoref{fig:deap_hyperparams_grouped} depicts the relative distribution of hyperparameters grouped by instances scoring above and below the average f1-score. In these diagrams, it becomes even more significant how well the sigmoid kernel performs as nearly every model above average uses this function. Besides that, the grouping also make more insights about the feature count visible. The diagram suggests that, one the one hand, most models that perform above the average f1-score use few features (around two to 20 builds the majority). One the other hand, models performing below average tend to use more features, as 35\% make use of the maximum available amount of features being 50. Another interesting result is that the choice of the parameter C doesn't show many differences between above and below average performance, as the distribution in both cases looks similar with a peak at $C = 10.0$. As the \textit{rbf} kernel is not utilized much, the choice for a specific $\gamma$ value does not give away any trend towards good or bad performance. \par
